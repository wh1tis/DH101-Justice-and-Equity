Within Stanford's Educational Opportunity Project, the Educational Opportunity Trends Project displays the education performance of students in the United States from 2009 to 2025. Students are grouped and divided according to their race, gender, the state in which they reside, and their families economic status. Furthermore, their scores on national assessments in subjects such as Math and RLA are also revealed. This dataset can exhibit relationships between each student's various subgroups and their assessment scores. Events where there are significant differences in scores between years or between subjects can be illuminated.
	The researchers used data from the Department of Education’s National Center for Education Statistics (NCES), which receives data from the EDFacts Database. The researchers used the restricted files from this database to approximate test score distributions in districts, counties, administrative school districts, and commuting zones. In the data, schools are identified by NCES IDs that are linked to more data from The Common Core of Data (CCD). Using the CCD guidelines, researchers create the SEDA school ID. To create stable geographic identifiers, researchers used the school’s, commuting zone’s, administrative district’s, and county’s most recent geographical information. Additionally, researchers used heteroskedastic ordered probit modeling to approximate the proficiency thresholds within each state, grade, year, and subject. The researchers then estimate the model using the counts of all students in each geographic school district within the state, grade, year, and subject. 
The Educational Opportunity Project at Stanford made these datasets, and each was funded separately. The Stanford Education Data Archive (SEDA) and the Trends dataset were funded by the Institute of Education Sciences, the Spencer Foundation, the William T. Grant Foundation, the Bill and Melinda Gates Foundation, and the Overdeck Family Foundation, with a visiting scholar fellowship from the Russell Sage Foundation. The Segregation Tracking Project was funded by the Russell Sage Foundation, the Robert Wood Johnson Foundation, and the Bill and Melinda Gates Foundation. The Early Childhood dataset was funded by the Heising-Simons Foundation, the Valhalla Foundation, and the Overdeck Family Foundation. Since the Trends data we are critiquing has the same funders as SEDA, the relevant sponsors are a mix of a federal agency and private philanthropies. Private funding does not mean the data is biased. But, some of these foundations like Gates and Overdeck, have stated education reform agendas so it's worth asking how that might affect what gets measured.
The data reveals nothing about school level practices. We have no information on accelerated classes, GATE and gifted programs, tutoring, or other opportunities afforded to students. We can create correlations between demographics, geography, and performance, but explaining that performance is not possible with this data alone. Because data is grouped, conclusions about individuals would be an ecological fallacy. There is no nativity variable or multiracial category. Nativity would strengthen claims about redlining and other historic practices. The 4 racial categories flatten people who may come from nuanced communities with unique racial identities. Only public school data is available, which creates selection bias if we compare groups with uneven private school enrollment. We lack information outside of grades 3-8. So, we cannot draw conclusions about grades K-2 and 9-12 outcomes. Importantly, we have no information on regional cultural perceptions about what qualifies as a "good" school or what educational outcomes are desirable. Individual state tests are put on a single scale using results from the NAEP, which is only administered in 4th and 8th grade every other year. Other grades and years are estimated, and 2025 was extrapolated. The data only includes information on math and reading/language arts scores, which excludes proficiencies that may be perceived as important in different communities. Gender is binary in the dataset, erasing non-binary students. 
Many states have incomplete data due to opt outs and gaps in testing; New York has no data after 2014. We have no data for 2020 and 2021. The dataset lacks subgroups for students with disabilities or English learners, and there are no intersectional subgroups. For example, there is no subgroup for economically disadvantaged Black students. The “scores” are model estimates of the mean created through HETOP with raw numbers of students in each proficiency group, which are determined by the states. Small groups are excluded,  disproportionately removing rural and Native American students. Virtual, special education, and vocational schools are excluded from 2022 on. States report race, gender, and ECD themselves. ECD is defined by states, usually based on students with free or reduced price lunch eligibility. This further weakens state-by-state comparison. Because students are not tracked individually, mobility of underperforming students may make a school's learning rate appear higher than it is.. The SEDA team found that boys do relatively better on multiple choice questions, whereas girls perform better on written responses. Many states shifted to Common Core tests in 2015, which include more written response questions. Shifts in gender gaps may reflect changes in format.
