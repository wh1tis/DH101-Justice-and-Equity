Annotated Bibliography
Song, Qiongjiang, et al. “Effects of Family Socioeconomic Status on Educational Outcomes in Primary and Secondary Education: A Systematic Review of the Causal Evidence.” Educational Psychology Review [New York], vol. 37, no. 2, no. 29, June 2025, https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-025-10004-8.
In the scholarly article, Song investigates the general belief that socioeconomic status (SES) plays a significant role in a student's educational outcomes. He states that SES does have an impact on educational outcomes, but not as much as previous studies suggest. A student's educational outcome is measured in academic achievement and educational attainment. Song emphasizes a clear difference between them. The former is represented through ‘standardized tests or grades, which capture performance at specific points in time’(28), while the latter is measured over a much longer period of time, and is represented by the highest level of education that an individual reaches. In this specific study, parental education and family income are the main indicators used. Evidence shows that the effect that SES has on academic achievement is a value of Cohen's d = 0.03, and the effect that SES has on educational attainment is a value of Cohen's d = 0.08. Both of these values are quite small, demonstrating relatively limited direct causal influence that SES has on educational outcomes. With that in mind, Song found that parental education had a larger effect on a student’s educational attainment compared to family income. Song states that there are alternative factors that might also have an impact on educational outcomes. One of these potential factors refers to a study done on the Transmission of Cognitive Ability (Marks & O’Connell, 2023; O’Connell & Marks, 2022). Song considers the possibility of impact that the genetic and environmental transmission of cognitive ability may have. This is not to say that SES has no effect on educational outcomes. Simply that the causal influence of family income and parental education on SES is relatively small. Parents with higher education “often have greater knowledge and professional skills to support their children’s development” (27). In other words, family income and parental education mean students have more access to things that actually do have direct correlation to educational outcomes. Qiongjiang Song is a Doctor of Psychology at the University of Hong Kong, who specializes in social justice in education. Even though Song resides in Hong Kong, his research is mainly based on papers written in English so it is still relevant because our research questions pertain to students grade 3-8 in the United States. In both developed and developing countries, Song finds that this notion remains consistent, just with varying magnitudes of impact. This scholarly article holds quite a large amount of weight in relation to our project because it looks at socioeconomic status in a way that challenges previous research. This should shape the way our research question is worded, and should be a factor to consider if we propose any policies.
Vinopal, Katie, and Taryn W. Morrissey. “Neighborhood Disadvantage and Children’s Cognitive Skill Trajectories.” Children and Youth Services Review [England], vol. 116, no. 105231, September 2020, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.childyouth.2020.105231.
In the scholarly article, Vinopal and Morrissey discuss the relationship between neighborhood poverty and academic achievement. The data set that they use is the 2011 Early Childhood Longitudinal Study-Kindergarten Cohort, which contains information regarding the math and reading assessments of students from kindergarten through 3rd grade. Something that is essential to note is that rather than measuring family poverty (or SES), the authors measure neighborhood poverty. Neighborhood poverty looks at the percentage of individuals living under the official federal poverty line in a geographical area.
Vinopal and Morrissey conclude that students who are economically disadvantaged score lower than other students in both math and reading, in all grades. At the beginning of Kindergarten, the difference in the math scores between the two groups was around 0.6, which correlates to half a year of learning. In the sample set, White and Asian students typically lived in lower poverty areas while Black and Hispanic students live in higher poverty areas. From fall to spring, scores of kindergarteners in low poverty neighborhoods “grew an average of 0.64 in math and 0.78 points in reading” (5). On the other hand, scores of kindergarteners in moderately  high poverty areas  grew 0.79 in math and 0.85 in reading. Notably, the authors state that first grade is a key point for any potential intervention for students of high neighborhood poverty. Additional resources or tutoring could potentially improve their academic achievement. Vinopal and Morrissey compare neighborhood poverty in both urban and suburban areas. In high poverty neighborhoods in both rural and urban locations, students both “show catch up patterns” (7), with rural students catching up to a lesser extent. In the grades after that, there is slightly more improvement for students living in rural areas compared to urban areas in mathematics. For urban students in high neighborhood poverty areas, there is a slightly larger growth in reading scores. Katie Vinopal is an Associate Professor in The John Glenn College of Public Affairs at Ohio State University with an expertise in poverty and inequality as well as educational policy. Taryn Morrissey is a Professor in the Department of  Public Administration and Policy at American University, and she specifically researches policies in education for children. Both individuals have strong backgrounds in this field and appear to be credible. This scholarly article is helpful in two ways. First, it shows that there is a difference in academic achievement between low and high neighborhood poverty students. Second, it relates to one of our research questions where we inquire whether or not there is a difference in academic achievement between urban and rural areas in the United States.
Minh, Anita, et al. “A Review of Neighborhood Effects and Early Child Development: How, Where, and for Whom, Do Neighborhoods Matter?” Health & Place [England], vol. 46, July 2017, pp. 155–74, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.healthplace.2017.04.012.
In the scholarly article, Minh conducts a scoping review of 42 studies on how location relates to the development of children aged 0-6. Minh concludes that the data collected shows evidence that “social inequalities between neighborhoods are related to variability in early childhood development” (17). Neighborhood mechanisms such as safety and potential interventions are possibly related to development. Similarly, a family environment influences an individual's early childhood progression. Further, she acknowledges that there is an association between neighborhood mechanisms and family environments. She also admits that there is not nearly enough research done to conclude whether or not there is a “developmental impact of social divisions between neighborhoods by gender, ethnicity and race” (17). However, she does point out that children may not be able to get the resources/help that they need due to potential prejudice that comes from residential segregation. Out of the 42 studies analyzed, only 2 failed to find evidence that explained the relationship between neighborhood SES and the outcomes of a child. The problems in behavior were found to have a relationship with the kind of parenting practices used. Out of the 6 studies conducted with “place-based factors that may moderate the relationship between” (16), neighborhood environment and development, one of them looked at exposure to a toxin (PAH) during their prenatal period and neighborhood SES. They found that children with a higher level of prenatal exposure had lower cognitive performance at age 5 compared to those who did not. A majority of  the studies that Minh examines are done in the United States, with the others being done in similarly developed countries. Furthermore, Minh is a credible source because she has a PhD in Population and Public Health at the University of British Columbia and is currently an Assistant Professor at Oregon Health and Science University. Also, her work was published on the peer reviewed journal Health and Place. Although Minh's research only spans the course of an individual's early childhood, it provides a strong foundation for our group's claim that geographical location is a determinant in a child's academic outcomes. One of our main research questions discusses the performance gap between Black and White students in “progressive” areas. The study regarding the toxin PAH takes place in New York City (a predominantly progressive city) with African American children. Although it states that neighborhood SES and exposure to the toxins are different, the two factors can still overlap.
Matheny, Kaylee T., et al. “Uneven Progress: Recent Trends in Academic Performance Among U.S. School Districts.” American Educational Research Journal, vol. 60, no. 3, 2023, pp. 447–85, https://doi.org/10.3102/00028312221134769.
Matheny, Thompson, Townley-Flores, and Reardon examine how academic achievement changed across American public school districts in the decade before the COVID-19 pandemic. Drawing on the Stanford Education Data Archive (SEDA), they describe district-level trends in average achievement between 2009 and 2019, using standardized test scores from third through eighth grade in math and English language arts. The authors’ central argument is that national averages conceal enormous local variation. While district test scores rose only very slightly on average (roughly 0.001 standard deviations per year), districts differed significantly from one another; scores climbed in some districts and fell in others, and the same was true of achievement gaps. Turning to equity, the authors find that the average gaps between poor and non-poor students and between White and Black students widened over the period, while the gap between White and Hispanic students narrowed. For example, they find no evidence that districts face either a synergy or a tradeoff between raising overall achievement and reducing disparities, since gains in average performance were unrelated to trends in gaps. In their exploratory analysis of what drives these patterns, the strongest predictors of changing achievement gaps were the levels and trends in within-district racial and economic segregation, along with shifts in unequal access to certified teachers. From this, they suggest, in line with other research, that reducing segregation and inequality within districts may promote greater equality of educational opportunity.
This study is useful for research on educational inequality because it challenges the assumption that steady national progress means progress for all students. Furthermore, it locates the forces behind improvement and decline at the local level and also adds to work on the income achievement gap and research linking school segregation to racial achievement gaps, while adding a decade-long, nationwide district-level perspective. The work’s greatest strength is its scale and methodological care, since by placing state tests from across the country on a common scale, the authors can compare virtually every district in the nation. However, the analysis is descriptive and correlational, so it cannot establish that segregation or teacher access actually causes gaps to widen, a limitation the authors themselves acknowledge by framing that portion as exploratory. The reliance on standardized tests in grades three through eight also leaves out high school outcomes and non-test measures of learning. Lastly, because the data end in 2019, the study captures a pre-pandemic baseline, which makes it valuable for comparison with post-COVID research but limits what it can say about current conditions.
Clayton, Grant, et al. “Characteristics of the Opt-Out Movement: Early Evidence for Colorado.” Education Policy Analysis Archives, vol. 27, no. 33, 2019, https://doi.org/10.14507/epaa.27.4126.
Clayton, Bingham, and Ecks examine the opt-out movement, in which families refuse state-mandated standardized tests, and ask which schools it affected most. They trace the movement to the 2015 rollout of the Common Core-aligned PARCC exams, which sparked a backlash against test-based accountability. Colorado was a striking case: no district fell below the required 95 percent participation rate between 2011 and 2014, yet in 2015 some districts dropped to as low as 60 percent. Participation fell at every grade but most sharply in high school, where ninth-grade rates dropped from 98 to 75 percent. Using statewide school-level ELA participation data for grades 3 through 9 from 2012 to 2016, the authors ran linear regressions on urbanicity, racial composition, accountability rating, charter status, and poverty. Their primary argument is that opt-out was concentrated in charter schools, suburban and rural areas, higher-performing schools, and schools with more White students, while schools with more Black and Hispanic students saw relative gains in participation. They attribute this to cultural capital, by which affluent, informed parents feel empowered to challenge school requirements, while others defer to schools or may not know opting out is possible. They find no evidence that low-rated schools pushed weaker students to opt out to inflate scores, though they warn that relaxing participation rules could invite such strategic behavior.
This study matters for research on educational equity because it suggests the test data behind school ratings, teacher evaluations, and large-scale research may not be representative of the students schools actually serve. It connects directly to Matheny et al.’s “Uneven Progress,” which measures district achievement trends with state test scores; if high-performing, predominantly White schools are underrepresented after 2015, such estimates could be biased. It also reinforces survey research by Pizmony-Levy and Saraisky profiling opt-out parents as largely affluent and White. The study’s strengths are its panel design, which the authors call a first in this literature, and a falsification test using a pre-opt-out year. However, the authors admit that when all variables enter a single model, most effects lose significance and some reverse direction, so the results are not robust to specification. Only the elementary charter decline and the relative increases in schools with more Black and Hispanic students hold consistently, making the headline claims weaker than the abstract implies. Because the data are school-level, the cultural capital explanation remains an interpretation rather than a tested finding. The study also covers only two opt-out years and reports only ELA results, offering early evidence rather than a settled account.
Reardon, Sean F., et al. “The Geography of Racial/Ethnic Test Score Gaps.” American Journal of Sociology, vol. 124, no. 4, 2019, pp. 1164–221.
Reardon, Kalogrides, and Shores map how white-Black and white-Hispanic achievement gaps vary across American communities and ask what local conditions explain that variation. Drawing on roughly 200 million standardized math and reading test scores from grades 3 through 8 between 2009 and 2013, they estimate gaps for thousands of school districts and nearly 400 metropolitan areas. They argue that gaps are not a fixed national feature but a product of local context. Average district gaps were about 2/3 of a standard deviation for white-Black comparisons and half a standard deviation for white-Hispanic comparisons, but they ranged from nearly zero to more than 1.2 standard deviations, and state differences explained only a small share of that variation. Economic, demographic, segregation, and schooling characteristics together explain roughly 1/2 to 3/4 of the geographic variation. The strongest correlates are racial differences in parental income and education, overall parental education levels, and segregation, especially racial differences in exposure to high-poverty schools rather than racial isolation alone. School resources such as per-pupil spending and class size explained very little. The authors conclude that gaps are best understood as markers of local inequality in educational opportunity, shaped partly by how residential and school segregation channel family advantage.
This article is foundational for research on educational inequality because it shifts the focus from national averages to the local structures that produce disparities. It provides the framework behind later Stanford Education Data Archive work, including Matheny et al.’s “Uneven Progress,” which similarly finds that segregation predicts whether gaps widen or narrow over time. Read alongside Clayton et al.’s study of the opt-out movement, it also raises a caution: if test participation varies by race and school affluence, gap estimates built on state tests may be distorted. The study’s major strength is its unprecedented scale and its method for placing different state tests on a common scale, which allows nationwide comparisons that were previously impossible. Its emphasis on differential exposure to poverty also refines the segregation debate by suggesting that concentrated poverty, not racial composition itself, does much of the harm. However, the analysis is descriptive, so it cannot show that segregation causes gaps; segregation and income inequality are tightly intertwined and difficult to separate. Because many districts have too few Black or Hispanic students, only about a quarter of districts could be estimated, leaving smaller and more homogeneous communities underrepresented. Gaps are also relative measures, so a small gap may reflect low achievement for all groups, as in Detroit. Finally, school quality measures are crude, and a substantial share of variation remains unexplained.
Howell, Junia, and Alannah Caisey. “What We Need Is Education: Differentiating the Mechanisms Contributing to Persistent Racial Inequality of Education.” Phylon (1960-), vol. 56, no. 1, 2019, pp. 58–80. JSTOR, https://www.jstor.org/stable/26743831. Accessed 23 Oct. 2026.
Howell et al. argues that despite efforts in educational equality, U.S. public schools remain separate and unequal. They note that previous studies have not examined the blend of mechanisms contributing to academic inequality such as residential segregation and racialized teaching practices. The authors used a Du Boisian empirical sociology equation to analyze descriptive parameters to expose how each mechanism shapes racial inequality in Advanced Placement test scores. The results showed that inequality in Black students and other students of color is mostly due to district and school level differences. On the other hand, inequality between Black and White is primarily due to intra-school dynamics such as teacher-student interactions. The first part of the article delves into the historical timeline of Blacks achieving access to education beginning in the 1800s. The article then goes on to discuss how despite the integration of school, segregation still remains via academic tracking. Academic tracking is when starting in kindergarten, students are placed on different academic “tracks” determining which curriculum they will follow. In addition, the authors explain ethnographic studies that prove that teachers’ racial biases affect how they interact with their students and what material they assign. Additionally, the authors discuss how Black and Latinx students receive harsher punishments for comparable offenses to White students. Next, the authors used the Du Bois based equation based on the ethnographic studies to identify the proportion of overall inequality due to each mechanism. For evidence, the authors used the Department of Education’s 2013-2024 Civil Rights Data Collection school-level data. Additionally, they used the rate of students passing the Advanced Placement (AP) tests. The final section of the article explains the results. The authors found that despite U.S. public schools having more students of color, this diversity is not reflected in students passing Advanced Placement tests (21). The authors hypothesize that this inequality is due to White and Asian students disproportionately living in well-resourced school districts, tracked into AP courses, and treated more favorably within the classroom. More specifically, the authors found that 49% of the inequality between White and Black students is between students within the same classrooms (21). The article concludes with a discussion of W.E.B. Du Bois’s and Dr. Martin Luther King Jr.’s perspectives on the education of Black youths. Overall, this article is important for our research regarding racial inequality in the U.S..
Logan, John R., et al. “The Geography of Inequality: Why Separate Means Unequal in American Public Schools.” Sociology of Education, vol. 85, no. 3, 2012, pp. 287–301. JSTOR, http://www.jstor.org/stable/41507163. Accessed 23 Oct. 2026.
In this article, authors John Logan et al. discusses the extent of disparities in academic performance between schools primarily attended by whites and Asians compared to Blacks, Hispanics, and Native Americans. Furthermore, the authors analyze the geography of school inequality. They analyze this inequality in two ways. Firstly, they examine the segregation of students based on racial composition, poverty, and metropolitan location. Secondly, it examines and estimates the effects of these and other school characteristics on school performance and identifies which aspects of school segregation are the most prevalent sources of disadvantage. The article begins by introducing the authors’ main research question which is whether children of different racial backgrounds attend different schools. The researchers assume that it is advantageous to attend a school where more students are successful. The authors use the No Child Left Behind Act, which introduced mechanisms to identify failing schools. Further, they use the testing requirement from the act to account for national performance disparities in schools attended by white and minority children. The next section of the article discusses the authors’ methodology. Using scores from 2004, they looked at 81,437 elementary, middle, and high schools for reading performance and 78,320 for math performance. The researchers found that Black, Hispanic, and Native American students were significantly more likely to attend low-performing schools compared to white and Asian students (13). It was found that Black, Hispanic, and Native American children attend schools that are on average at the 35th-40th percentile of performance, while White and Asian children are in schools around the 60th percentile (13). Additionally, researchers found that schools with higher concentrations of low-income students had lower academic performances. Furthermore, researchers found that poverty had an even greater effect on school performance than racial composition (14). This finding suggests that economic factors provide somewhat of an explanation in academic inequality. Finally, researchers found that being in an urban, suburban, or rural area did not independently explain school performance once poverty and racial composition were considered (13). The authors conclude that American public schools remain separate and unequal and argue that educational inequality is prevalent across the entire range of school performance, not just among the lowest performing schools. Overall, the article demonstrates that educational inequality is not only an issue of individual student ability, but is also connected to larger social and economic structures in the U.S..
Schaie, K. Warner. School Achievement of Children by Demographic and Socioeconomic Factors, United States. National Center for Health Statistics, 1971. Accessed 25 Oct. 2026.
This report by K. Warner Schaie delves into how demographic and socioeconomic characteristics are associated with achievement among children in the U.S.. It connects to research on educational opportunity because it highlights how inequalities in family resources and demographic backgrounds can contribute to differences in academic performance. More specifically, the research focuses on children aged 6-11 and investigates whether factors such as family income, parental education, race, sex, and geographic location are related to differences in reading and math performances. The article then discusses where the data is from. The study used data collected from the 1963-1965 National Health Examination Survey from the National Center for Health Statistics. The survey includes a nationally representative sample of 7,119 children. Children’s academic achievement was measured using the Wide Range Achievement Test (WRAT). Then, the researchers compared test results among different demographic and socioeconomic groups to determine patterns in academic achievement. Researchers found that family income and parental education were very important factors in determining children's academic performance. Additionally, significant differences in achievement were found across the demographic and socioeconomic variables examined. In regards to geography, the researchers found that children living in the midwest and northeast performed better than children in the south, especially in reading (4). Furthermore, girls generally performed better than boys in reading, while performances in math were relatively similar (4). Overall, the report highlights how family income, parental education, race, and geographic location are connected to differences in academic performance, and significant differences in achievement were found across the demographic and socioeconomic variables examined. This report is useful for understanding educational inequality because it demonstrates that academic achievement cannot be explained solely by individual ability or effort. After discussing the results, researchers reflect that a major limitation of the report is that the findings reflect the conditions of the historical period of 1963-1965. However, this also allows readers to see an early example of demographic and socioeconomic inequalities reflected in educational inequalities. The report provides a foundation for understanding how family resources and social circumstances relate to educational achievement in the United States while incorporating a historical perspective.
Kim, Youngmi, and Michael Sherraden. “Do Parental Assets Matter for Children’s Educational Attainment?: Evidence from Mediation Tests.” WashU Scholarly Repository, 1 Jan. 1970, repository.library.washu.edu/csd_research/178/.
The article by Youngmi Kim and Michael Sherraden examines the impact of parents’ wealth on children' s educational attainment. They investigate the effects of parents’ financial and nonfinancial assets on children’s high school completion, college attendance, and degree attainment. They explore how, “A low level of educational attainment often translates into less income and unstable employment in the labor market over the life course” (1). They compared families of similar income with different amounts of accumulated wealth, and found that even with similar incomes, families had vastly different levels of financial security. The authors used data from 632 participants from the Child and Young Adult data supplement to the National Longitudinal Study of Youth 1979 (NLSY79). In addition to the effect of parental financial and nonfinancial assets, the authors examined the effects of several other variables including parental involvement, children’s educational expectations, and children’s self esteem.
Youngmi Kim and Michael Sherraden examined the relationship between parental wealth and children’s educational attainment in a scholarly article. They focused on two types of assets. Financial assets, such as cash stocks and bonds, and non-financial assets, such as home ownership, cars, and other personal possessions. They examined their effects on three measures of education attainment. Completing high school, attending college, and attaining a college degree. Using data from the Child and Young Adult data supplement to the National Longitudinal Study of Youth 1979 (NLSY79), the authors found that children of parents with high levels of financial assets and home-ownership had higher levels of educational achievement than their counterparents with parents who had lower levels of these assets. The effects of family income were found to be insignificant when measures of specific assets and liabilities were included in the model. However, the non-financial assets and income has some significant effects on college degree attainment. Children’s educational expectations were found to mediate the effect of financial assets on high school completion. And the results of the study support the development of asset-building programs and policies that help to promote long-term educational attainment.
This article is relevant to my research in multiple areas. For one, it shows good empirical evidence of the effect of parental wealth on a child’s educational outcomes. Secondly, the article highlights that the effect of financial assets on their children’s education is not confined to the ability of parents to pay for their children’s tuition and other school-related expenses, such as books and tutoring. The article also shows the effects of financial assets on their children’s educational expectation, and on their confidence to pursue higher education, and their perception of what they can achieve. Both are important in relation to their academic achievement. The article further supports our thesis that accumulated wealth has an effect on children’s education.
Pfeffer, Fabian. “Growing Wealth Gaps in Education.” Demography, vol. 55, no. 3, 2018, pp. 1033–1068, read.dukeupress.edu/demography/article-abstract/55/3/1033/167883/Growing-Wealth-Gaps-in-Education.
In the article “Growing Wealth Gaps in Education”, Fabian Pfeffer explores the impact of family wealth on the educational attainment of children. Pfeffer utilized data from the Panel Study of Income Dynamics (PSID), a nationally representative sample of families, to examine the educational achievement of children born in 1970 and 1989. The author followed the educational attainment of children at ages 20 and 25 of the two birth cohorts, with over 5,000 children and their parents participating in the study. In order to measure family wealth, the author utilized the children’s parents’ net worth, which included savings, property, investments, and debt.
According to Pfeffer, students from the wealthiest families and those from the poorest families achieve very different levels of education. For example, students from the bottom quintile of wealth have a high school graduation rate of 72.8%, while those from the top quintile have a rate of 91.1%. This is a difference of 18.3 percentage points, proving there is a visual gap between wealth and education results. When looking at higher education, there are even larger differences between the groups of students. Only 21.4% of students from the poorest group of families attend college, while 53.5% of students from the wealthiest families do so. In terms of college graduation, there is a big difference between the two groups of students with only 9.1% of students from the poorest families graduating from college, compared to 53.7% of students from the wealthiest families. This is a difference of 44.6 percentage points. Pfeffer found that even when controlling family income, family wealth has an independent effect on educational achievement. Such as, the difference in the likelihood of earning a bachelor’s degree between students from the poorest and wealthiest families is 10.5 percentage points. He also found that the gap in college graduation rates between the poorest and richest families has increased from 39.5 percentage points for children born in the 1970s to 48.9 percentage points for children born in the 1980s. He describes this as a “large and rapidly increasing wealth gap in college attainment.” (1)
This article is useful for my research as it directly approves our thesis point. The statistics provided to support the claim that students from wealthy families are more likely to complete high school, attend college, and graduate from college than their less wealthy peers are relevant to my research. Next, the article provides evidence that the educational inequality is growing from generation to generation, and that the inequality is particularly pronounced with respect to college graduation. Students from wealthy families have increasing advantages over students from less wealthy families as more and more wealth is required to attend college. This article supports our research as it describes how wealth affects education, and how unequal finances can create unequal opportunities for education.
Levine, Phillip B., and Dubravka Ritter. “The Racial Wealth Gap, Financial Aid, and College Access.” NBER, 26 Sept. 2022, www.nber.org/papers/w30490.
Phillip B. Levine and Dubravka Ritter explore how differences in family wealth, especially across different racial groups, can affect a student’s ability to pay for, and attend college. In “The Racial Wealth Gap, Financial Aid, and College Access,” Levine and Ritter examine how the federal financial aid system treats certain aspects of families, such as retirement savings and home equity, which are not counted in the determination of a family’s ability to pay for college. The authors rely on data from three sources. The 2019 Survey of Consumer Finances (SCF), the 2015-2016 National Postsecondary Student Aid Study (NPSAS), and the Panel Study of Income Dynamics (PSID). Levine and Ritter examine how differences in family wealth affect the financial aid that students are eligible to receive, as well as their chances of enrolling in college, the types of institutions that they attend, their chances of completing a degree, as well as the amount of student debt that they accumulate. The authors explain that, “race-neutral processes can generate disparate impacts” (3), because of the unequal financial circumstances of different racial groups.
Authors Levine and Ritter find that by excluding retirement savings and home equity from financial aid calculations, that financial aid creates an unfair advantage for the wealthiest families. White families are more likely to hold these assets than Black and Hispanic families with similar incomes. The median reduction in expected family contribution for White students from families with incomes between $75,000 and $125,000 was $5,600 compared to $2,800 for Black students with the same family income. For students from families with incomes between $125,000 and $200,000, the reduction in expected family contributions for White students was $15,600 compared to $3,700 for Black students. The researchers estimate that 851,000 students benefit from this policy each year, 581,000 of whom receive some institutional grant aid. The average annual subsidy is $4,100 for White students, $1,900 for Black students, and $3,300 for Hispanic students. The total value of these subsidies is $2.3 billion, 78.9% of which goes to White students. Levine and Ritter find that the advantage of the financial aid policy can explain 10% to 15% of the advantage that White students have over Black and Hispanic students in terms of college educational outcomes.
This article is important to our research question in a couple ways. The article provides some numerical data evidence that students from families with different amounts of accumulated wealth will pay different amounts for college, and receive different amounts of financial assistance. This article also shows evidence that wealth inequality contributes to racial inequality in terms of educational opportunities, even when families have similar incomes. Students from wealthier families will have access to more financial assistance, more affordable colleges, and less student debt than students from less wealthy families. This article brings further evidence that supports my thesis that wealth and money can determine whether or not students can go to college, what types of college they can afford to attend, and how they will be affected by their financial circumstances while in college.
Castellano, Katherine E., et al. “Exploring Differential Change in Student Performance during the COVID-19 Pandemic across the United States.” Large-Scale Assessments in Education [New York], vol. 13, no. 1, no. 11, December 2025, https://doi.org/10.1186/s40536-025-00242-1.
Castellano and her colleagues argue that the COVID-19 pandemic widened existing gaps in student performance, with the biggest losses among Black and Hispanic students, students eligible for free or reduced price lunch, and students in schools that move any classes online. They compare 2020 and 2022 NAEP Long Term Trend scores for 9 year olds, for the most part fourth graders, in roughly 330 public schools sampled in both years, using school fixed effects so each student is compared with peers in the same school. Within schools, Black students lost about 10 more math points than White students and Hispanics students about 4 more, while schools with at least some remote classes dropped about 7 more points in math and 8 more in reading than schools that stayed in person. This is important because most pandemic studies lean on convenience samples or state tests switched onto one scale, where this one uses a single nationally representative examination given right before and after the shutdowns. The authors say the estimates are not casual, that their remote learning measures is a rough administrator survey question that about 29 percent of schools skipped, and that it covers only one age group. For our project, it adds a post pandemic perspective on the race and income gaps in grades 3 to 8, and its results speak directly to our urban vs rural question.
Reardon, Sean F., et al. “Gender Achievement Gaps in U.S. School Districts.” American Educational Research Journal [Los Angeles, CA], vol. 56, no. 6, December 2019, pp. 2474–508, https://doi.org/10.3102/0002831219843824.
Reardon and his partners argue that gender achievement gaps are not a fixed national fact but vary a lot from one school district to another, and that part of that variation tracks local socioeconomic conditions. They estimate male to female gaps in math and English language arts for nearly 10,000 districts using about 290 million state test scores from grades 3 through 8, placed on a common scale with NAEP, and then regress those gaps on district income, education, and occupation measures. The average district has almost no math gap but an ELA gap of about 0.23 standard deviations favoring girls, roughly two thirds of a grade level, and math gaps lean more male favoring in wealthier districts and in districts with larger adult gender disparities, which together explain about a fifth of the variation in math gaps but almost none in ELA. This is important because it shows that even a gap people assume is universal depends on place and community resources, the same logic behind our focus on geography. The authors stress that the results are correlational, and most of the district-to-district variation stays unexplained, so the study describes patterns rather than causes. Gender is not the center of our research, but this source gives us a scale for comparison, and it uses the same kind of nationwide district test data as those sources, which strengthens the case for using that data to compare places.
Drescher, Jessica, et al. “The Geography of Rural Educational Opportunity.” RSF: Russell Sage Foundation Journal of the Social Sciences [New York], vol. 8, no. 3, May 2022, pp. 123–49, https://doi.org/10.7758/RSF.2022.8.3.05.
The authors argue that rural and nonrural students look similar on average, but that this hides large differences by student group and by where a rural district sits. They use about 430 million test scores from the Stanford Education Data Archive for roughly 12,500 districts, including more than 6,500 rural ones, to compare third grade achievement and third-to-eighth-grade learning rates. Rural and nonrural students differ by only about 0.01 standard deviations overall, but rural White and Native American students score about half a grade level below their nonrural peers, economically disadvantaged rural students score higher than disadvantaged nonrural students while wealthier rural students score lower, and family income predicts achievement less strongly in rural districts. Census division alone explains 23 percent of the variation in rural third grade scores, and community demographics explain more than half. This source matters because rural America is often left out of work on educational inequality, which tends to focus on cities and suburbs. The authors note that their analysis is descriptive, cannot explain mechanisms, and is limited to grades 3 through 8, and they find that the results shift depending on whether rural is defined by school or by district.
Goldhaber, Dan, et al. “The Consequences of Remote and Hybrid Instruction During the Pandemic.” NBER Working Paper No. 30010, National Bureau of Economic Research, 2022, pp. 1–4, 10–12, 14–15, www.nber.org/papers/w30010.
Goldhaber, et al. provide evidence that remote instruction related to the COVID-19 pandemic widened academic achievement gaps by race and poverty. High-poverty districts were more likely to go online and had worse outcomes when they did.
To assist in COVID recovery, the federal government committed $190 billion in aid to education agencies. Under the American Rescue Plan, districts are only required to spend 20% on academic recovery. In high poverty districts, all of the federal aid may be required to avoid impacts on intergenerational mobility and future earnings. The paper relies on data from 2.1 million students from 49 states and D.C., with students in grades 3-8 for the follow up year. MAP growth assessments (which have adaptive difficulty) sourced from the Growth Research Database are used to measure achievement. Common Core of Data is used to measure urbanicity, expenditures on secondary and elementary education, and participation in the federal Free and Reduced-Price Lunch Program. COVID infection rates by county are sourced from Johns Hopkins, density from the Census Bureau with estimates of federal Elementary and Secondary School Emergency Relief (ESSER) Funds by district. The authors model expected pre-pandemic growth and measure how far students fell short during the pandemic, by race, school poverty, and instructional mode. Where schools stayed in person, math gaps between high-poverty and low-poverty schools did not widen. Where schools were remote for more than half the year, students in high-poverty schools lost about 0.44 standard deviations, compared with 0.26 in low-poverty schools.
The paper provides key insights into pandemic impact on existing inequality in academic outcomes. The paper’s evidence strongly suggests the pandemic accelerated many of the negative impacts of socioeconomic inequality on educational achievement. So, patterns observed pre-pandemic may not hold afterward. Understanding how recent events intensified inequality can inform policy decisions and future research on recovery and improvement of equity in American education.
We can take inspiration from the methods, motivations, and analysis this paper demonstrates for many of our proposed research questions. Beyond the COVID time period, this research is relevant to any question pertaining to racial and economic equality during and after the pandemic. The Educational Opportunity Trends data continues past the pandemic, so these findings can be tested. If remote instruction widened poverty gaps, high-poverty districts should show larger score drops after 2019, and possibly slower recovery. Notably, the paper distinguishes between "disparate incidence” (high-poverty schools are more likely to go online) and “disparate impact” (remote learning hurt more for high-poverty districts). We should not treat the pandemic as one uniform shock to all districts. The paper uses MAP’s adaptive testing rather than state assessments, which could lead to different conclusions than our SEDA dataset.
Reardon, Sean F., et al. “Is Separate Still Unequal? New Evidence on School Segregation and Racial Academic Achievement Gaps.” Working Paper, 23 Sept. 2019, pp. 1–2, 27–30, 32–35, edopportunity.org/papers/reardon-segregation-working-paper-version-sept-23-2019.pdf.
Reardon et al. examine how segregation predicts racial academic achievement gaps. More segregated school systems have wider gaps at the third grade level and those gaps widen faster through eighth grade. A district or schools’ racial composition alone does not predict achievement gaps. Black and Hispanic students are more concentrated into high poverty schools within a district, and impoverished schools produce worse outcomes. “Separate is still unequal.”
Eight years of SEDA test data from every public US school district were used to construct cross-sectional and panel models that control for racial socioeconomic status differences and residential segregation. A 0.10 difference in exposure to minority schoolmates was associated with a 0.05-0.07 standard deviation larger third-grade racial gap. For a case comparison, NYC and Fulton County, Georgia have similar levels of segregation and Black-white family socioeconomic gaps, but Fulton has a 1.1 standard deviation gap in Black-white achievement, whereas NYC has a 0.77 standard deviation gap. The Black-white difference in school poverty rates is 22 percentage points in NYC, it is 52 percentage points in Fulton. This helps explain the difference in their gap. Segregation’s negative effects appear to primarily relate to poverty rates.
This paper relies on the same data set that our group is using, and claims that no district where minority students disproportionately attend high-poverty schools has a small gap in achievement.This should inform any minority-related research around educational achievement that we conduct. Importantly, we should consider school poverty as a pivotal factor in racial academic achievement gaps.
One of our research questions asks, “The gap in performance between Black and white elementary school students is largest in purportedly “progressive” areas, what causes this gap?” This paper gives us a clear lead to follow. These districts are generally affluent, so district wide poverty will not explain the gap. The mechanism defined in this paper works intra-district: what matters is whether Black students attend higher-poverty schools than their white counterparts. We can test whether these “progressive” districts with the largest achievement gaps also have the largest racial differences in exposure to school poverty. The paper offers guardrails for our analysis. Schoolmates' prior scores and racial composition are ruled out. Teacher experience and school resources, like gifted programs, are inconclusive because the provided measures of them are weak. We need to look outside our dataset if we want to investigate those causes.
Reardon, Sean F., et al. “Patterns and Trends in Racial/Ethnic and Socioeconomic Academic Achievement Gaps.” Draft chapter, Apr. 2014. Handbook of Research in Education Finance and Policy, edited by Helen F. Ladd and Margaret E. Goertz, 2nd ed., Stanford Center for Education Policy Analysis, pp. 1, 3–6, 14, 22, swap.stanford.edu/was/20151211033644mp_/http://cepa.stanford.edu/sites/default/files/reardon%20robinson-cimpian%20weathers%20HREFP%20chapter%20april2014.pdf.
Reardon et al. argue that racial and socioeconomic gaps are a “stubborn feature” of American education. These gaps were larger in the 1970s but persist in the current day at 0.5-1.0 standard deviations. Concerningly, the Black-white gap grows over school years in ways that socioeconomic status cannot explain, suggesting that schools are contributing to the problem. At the time of publishing, researchers struggled to explain the mechanisms behind these gaps.
Researchers used a synthesis of NAEP Long-Term Trend data from 1971, Main NAEP from 1990 and ECLS-K data. The Black-white gap narrowed from the 70s to 80s, then expanded in the early 90s, before shrinking since 1999. The income achievement gap, defined as the difference in average test scores from the 90th percentile in family income, and scores from families in the 10th percentile of family income, has grown roughly 40% in recent decades. This could result from rising income inequality in the same period and possible changes in family investment patterns. Socioeconomic status explained about 85% of the Black-white math gap in kindergarten but only about 60% by third grade (Fryer & Levitt). Reardon’s more recent paper (2019) builds on this research, citing the fact that Black-white gaps narrowed after desegregation. The paper provides data from a large time frame that helps bring clarity to the educational landscape we observe today. The researchers also make it clear that conclusions depend on the test metric used, which we must consider as a part of our own research. It’s important to note in our own project that racial education achievement gaps were narrowing in the time period before our primary dataset. The fact that gaps grow during school makes way for the school-poverty mechanism discussed in Reardon’s later work, which will be vital to our project. Our data can show whether progress continued, stalled, or reversed after 2009.
