Educational inequality in the United States is generally linked to school funding, community resources, and family income. Other influences on educational inequality include race and geographical location. Historical efforts to restrict educational opportunities for certain racial groups include laws such as the Civil Rights Act of 1964 and the Fair Housing Act of 1968. While these laws helped reduce open racial discrimination, this discrimination remained in education. Contemporary housing segregation and other inequalities impact student education. As scholar Sean Reardon notes, achievement gaps by race and socioeconomic status remain a “stubborn feature” of education in the United States (Patterns and Trends, 1). An important issue in educational policy is the extent to which policies promoting equal opportunity have been effective. This research project examines the relationship between race, class, and academic achievement in U.S. school districts. Data used for this project include resources from the Stanford Educational Opportunity Project and the Stanford Education Data Archive. Reardon states that “no district where minority students disproportionately attend high-poverty schools has a small gap in achievement” (Is Separate Still Unequal?”, 23). Such information indicates that school and residential segregation negatively impact academic achievement, even in wealthier areas. Additionally, Song states that “parents with higher education often have greater knowledge and professional skills to support their children’s development” (Effects of Family Socioeconomic, 27). In addition to family income, other factors, such as parental education, contribute to differences in academic achievement. 
Researchers generally agree that Black-white achievement gaps vary widely from district to district and are shaped by local conditions, especially racial differences in parental income and education and in segregation (Geography of Racial/Ethnic Test Score Gaps). Later SEDA research finds that racial composition alone does not predict gaps. What matters is whether Black students disproportionately attend high-poverty schools (Is Separate Still Unequal?). For example, New York City and Fulton County, Georgia have similar levels of segregation, but Fulton's gap is much larger because Black and white students there attend schools with very different poverty rates. The literature disagrees, however, about which mechanisms matter most. Logan et. al. found that school poverty outweighs race. Howell and Caisey however attribute nearly half of Black-white inequality to dynamics within classrooms, such as tracking and teacher bias (What We Need Is Education, 21). Pfeffer links family wealth to educational attainment, while Song argues that the direct causal effects of family socioeconomic status are smaller than once believed (Effects of Family Socioeconomic). However, there are still several questions that remain unanswered. Since most studies are descriptive and cannot establish causation, rural districts are often overlooked, and post-pandemic patterns may differ from earlier trends. None of the studies we reviewed examine whether a district’s political leaning is related to the size of its achievement gap, or whether white residents’ economic advantages explain that relationship, which is the focus of our project.
This study is significant because it helps us understand educational inequalities in more progressive areas with policies intended to increase diversity and inclusion. Despite efforts to make education more available and legal barriers to education less restrictive, large achievement gaps exist between school districts in the United States. By investigating these issues, our project aims to contribute to knowledge about racial achievement gaps, including those in areas with predominately liberal political attitudes. Research on racial achievement gaps may help other researchers understand the role of  economic and other school-related and political factors in producing racial achievement gaps. Comparisons of urban and rural school districts may add to knowledge about educational inequities by providing information on the effects of area characteristics on students' educational opportunities. Ultimately, this research is important because issues related to educational outcomes are broader social issues in the United States than issues specific to particular schools. Along with other advances in policies and practices to increase access to education, this research suggests a need to address ongoing disparities in educational opportunities for students of different races and economic backgrounds.
